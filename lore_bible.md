# The Temple of Rhys — Lore Bible

*Companion to `design_v2.md` (§3 World & lore, §4 Characters). Last updated 2026-10-16.*

This document decides **who and what is real inside the game's world**: which fictional characters actually lived, which real writers never existed, and which real writers existed but invented their characters. It also sets out how texts are used in the game. The quote collection it governs is **Found Writings** (`rhys_writings.md`, 2026-10-15). Every quote printed in-game, whether on a wall, in a book, or spoken by the professor, has to pass the rules here.

## How to read this document

The conventions are the same as in `design_v2.md`:

- **Plain text is decided.** Here "decided" has two sources, and the **Basis** columns say which applies:
  - **Eir:** stated directly by the author (§1, 2026-10-16).
  - **Canon:** forced by a decided rule plus something Lovecraft's own text, or a witness document (§2.1), actually says. A canon item can only be overturned by changing the rule it comes from.
- **`> Proposal:`** blocks are suggestions that haven't been accepted. Accept, change, or cut them.
- *(derived)* marks a rule this document worked out from the ground rules. It isn't stated anywhere, so confirm it.
- **Open:** items are unresolved. They are all collected again in §11.

## 1. Ground rules (decided)

1. **This game is part of Lovecraft's mythos.** Lovecraft's own stories are canon: their events happened and their people lived.
2. **The unknowable stays unknowable.** Later authors turned the mythos into a catalogue of gods, with names, ranks, families, and good and evil sides. We don't. The entities are incomprehensible, extra-dimensional, and maddening to see. They are referenced the way Lovecraft referenced them, almost in passing, as if the reader were also a scholar of the occult. Names Lovecraft uses (Cthulhu, Azathoth, Nyarlathotep…) are allowed, and his stories are canon. But **a player who never translates the script should not be able to tell that the game is set in Lovecraft's universe.**
3. **Historical authors and scholars are real**, and we may tweak the history of their work to tie it to the game. Example: Shelley's "Ozymandias" is a real poem by a real poet, but it may secretly be about a cult of Rhys. It might have been inspired by a real explorer who found a dead king's monument in the desert.
4. **For some books, the characters are real.** Van Helsing, the people of *King Solomon's Mines*, and Lovecraft's characters such as Abdul Alhazred are real in-world. Their words are attributed to them, never to the author who wrote the book in our world.
5. **Either the author is real or their characters are, never both.** Edgar Allan Poe is a real person whose writings were inspired by real accounts of the occult, so no character from Poe's works may be quoted as if they had lived.
6. **Every bearer becomes obsessed with the cults that came before.** Whoever holds the Fangs eventually starts scouring the world for everything written about Rhys and earlier cults. They found their own cult, build the perfect temple, and entomb themselves inside it as both sacrifice and guardian. That is why the temple holds historical and fictional texts, as books and as inscriptions on the walls.
7. **The temple can't be placed in time, and the professor knows more than its builder did.** The temple feels thousands of years old but couldn't have been built much before 1900. The professor can cite texts later than anything in the temple, as accounts scholars have access to, without those texts ever being in the script (§7).
8. **Rhys is not the only evil in the world.** Only one temple of Rhys is active at a time, but the world holds other horrors. Dracula can just be Dracula. Van Helsing is cited for his expertise on otherworldly forces and unknowable evils, not as someone who met a cult of Rhys.
9. **Ambiguity is preferred; contradiction is forbidden.** Witness journeys such as Quatermain's and Holly's really happened, but whether they had anything to do with Rhys is left unspecified. The temple's collection is a crazed person's search for any scrap of information, however tenuous. The professor's job is to be a source of knowledge for someone escaping a horrific situation. What we can never do is contradict ourselves: Van Helsing can't be real in one place and Bram Stoker real in another.
10. **The Fangs have no hard history.** We never say where the Fangs were, or who held them, in any specific place or with any specific person, and especially not in the last few hundred years. **Two fixed points:** at least one ancient Egyptian pyramid was built by a cult of Rhys. And, in the far distant past, the Fangs moved from Egypt to the British Isles (§6). The second is never stated in-game. It can only be inferred.
11. **Text for learning the script is different from text for lore.** Most text in the temple is rites, prayers and puzzle clues. Recognizable real writings are mostly there for meta reasons, so that players have phrases to grab onto when translating longer passages. Many short lines need no attribution and no justification inside the temple. They are easter eggs (§4).

## 2. Applying the binary

### 2.1 Every source has one in-world status

| Status | Means | Who exists | Attribute quotes to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Witness** | The book is a true account, written or compiled by the people in it. | Its characters. The real-world author **does not exist**. | The character who says or writes the words. |
| **Fiction** | A real author made it up. It may be inspired by real events. | The author. Their invented characters **never existed**. | The author. |
| **Record** | Nonfiction: history, essays, philosophy, scripture, and lyric poetry about no invented person. | The author, and any real people they describe. | The author. |
| **Legend** | Traditional or anonymous material: epics, folk tales, myth. | Uncertain in-world, exactly as uncertain as in ours, unless canon says otherwise. | The text, plus the translator where relevant. |
| **Pseudobook** | A book invented inside a Witness text, such as the *Necronomicon*. | The book and its in-world author. | Its in-world author. |

These statuses govern **lore texts**. Recognizable lines used without attribution are exempt (§4).

### 2.2 Witness status is opt-in *(derived)*
Any author this document doesn't list is **real**, and their work counts as Fiction or Record. A source becomes Witness only when that is decided and recorded in §3. This keeps the list of writers who don't exist short and deliberate.

### 2.3 Status belongs to the author, not the book
Under rule 5, an author either existed or didn't, so the status covers everything they wrote. If Haggard doesn't exist, *She* is a Witness account too, and Horace Holly is real (Eir uses Holly this way in §8). If Poe exists, Dupin and Pym are both fictional. §3.1 lists what this brings in for each absent author.

### 2.4 Real people inside fiction are still real *(derived)*
Rule 5 binds only **invented** characters. A Fiction work about a real person leaves that person real. Only the work's version of them is invented.

| Work (Fiction) | Real person underneath | In-world |
|---|---|---|
| Shakespeare, *Hamlet* | Amleth, from Saxo Grammaticus's legend | Legend, so whether he existed is uncertain. Hamlet as written is fictional. |
| Marlowe, *Doctor Faustus* | Johann Georg Faust (c. 1480–c. 1541) | Faust was real. Faustus, his bargain and his Mephistophilis are Marlowe's. |
| Shelley, "Ozymandias" | Ramesses II | Real. |
| Coleridge, "Kubla Khan" | Kublai Khan | Real. |
| Lovecraft, "Pickman's Model" (Witness) | Cotton Mather | Real, and he watched a witch hanged on Gallows Hill (Canon). |

### 2.5 Witnesses, not oracles *(derived)*
A Witness account is true **as its writers experienced it**, but their conclusions can be wrong. What they faced is never pinned to Rhys (rules 8–9): Van Helsing fought a vampire, and the game doesn't say it was anything else. Witnesses are cited for their testimony and their expertise about the uncanny in general (§8).

### 2.6 Real authors can carry true details (Canon)
Lovecraft supplies the precedent himself. In *At the Mountains of Madness*, Dyer treats Poe as a real author and wonders how Poe knew the cry *Tekeli-li*, which the Antarctic horrors really make. *Arthur Gordon Pym* is still fiction and Pym never lived, but one detail in it is true. Any Fiction source can work the same way. Spenser invented Guyon, and his house of Mammon is still unexpectedly accurate (§6).

### 2.7 Epigraphs have no in-world quoter *(derived)*
An epigraph at the head of a Lovecraft story is the absent author speaking, not a character. The quoted text is still real (Blackwood still wrote *The Centaur*), but nobody in-world chose it, and its attribution is whatever the true source is. This matters for the "Mixed" epigraphs (§9).

## 3. The register

### 3.1 Writers who do not exist

The world has no record of these three people: no birth, no career, no grave. Their books exist only as the documents their characters wrote.

| Writer | Why | In-world, the books are | What else this brings in (§2.3) | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **H. P. Lovecraft** | Rule 4: his characters are real | Manuscripts, notes, diaries and depositions by his narrators: Thurston's papers, Dyer's account, Peaslee's narrative | All his solo fiction (see **Open** below for the Dreamlands and the ghost-writing). His essays have no in-world author. | Eir |
| **Bram Stoker** | Rule 4: Van Helsing is real | **The Harker papers**: the journals, letters and phonograph diaries that Mina Harker typed up and put in order | *The Jewel of Seven Stars* (1903) and *The Lair of the White Worm* (1911) become Witness accounts too (§8) | Eir |
| **H. Rider Haggard** | Rule 4: the *King Solomon's Mines* cast is real | **Allan Quatermain's own memoir** (the book already presents itself this way, written in his own hand); **Horace Holly's manuscript** (*She*) | Every Quatermain adventure, and *She* and its sequels | Eir |

**Consequences to keep straight** *(derived)*

- **No Stoker means no *Dracula* in popular culture.** There's no famous novel, no Count in the films, no costume capes. Vampire folklore, Polidori's "The Vampyre" (1819) and Le Fanu's *Carmilla* (1872) still exist. The Harker papers are an obscure manuscript, so a professor who quotes Van Helsing is quoting a rare document that scholars of the occult can get hold of (§7.2), not a bestseller.
- **No Lovecraft means no Cthulhu in popular culture.** The name is known only to occult scholars and readers of rare manuscripts. That is rule 2 working in-world: the player recognizes the names, but the characters mostly don't.
- **Quatermain's memoir and Holly's manuscript were published in-world**, and readers took them for adventurers' tall tales. *(assumed; this is how the books frame themselves)*
- **Lovecraft's "History of the Necronomicon"** has no author, but its facts are canon (§3.5, §3.6). In-world they come from library catalogues and scholarly notes.

**Open:**
- Are the Dreamlands stories canon ("The Other Gods", "Sarnath", *Dream-Quest*)? Found Writings already quotes the Pnakotic Manuscripts and the Kadatheron cylinders from them. If they are canon, Randolph Carter really walked the Dreamlands.
- Are the other revisions and ghost-writing canon ("The Curse of Yig" for Zealia Bishop, "The Mound", "Out of the Aeons")? "Under the Pyramids" is settled (§3.2), but that settles nothing for the rest. "Yig" needs care if it comes in: its serpent god, "Father of Serpents", is the closest thing in Lovecraft to Rhys, and rule 2 forbids placing Rhys beside it.

### 3.6 Books that exist in-world

- **Lovecraft's own:** *Al Azif* / *Necronomicon*; the Pnakotic Manuscripts; the Seven Cryptical Books of Hsan; the brick cylinders of Kadatheron and the papyrus of Ilarnek. The last three depend on the Dreamlands decision.
- **Named by Lovecraft, invented by his friends:** *Book of Eibon*, von Junzt's *Unaussprechlichen Kulten*, Ludvig Prinn's *De Vermis Mysteriis*, the Comte d'Erlette's *Cultes des Goules*, the Eltdown Shards. **The titles exist, but nothing in them is ever quoted**, because every passage later written for them is someone else's.
- **Real books that canon treats as occult:** the Book of Dzyan (Blavatsky), Borellus, Mather's *Magnalia*, Murray, Frazer, Scott-Elliot.
- **Never:** the *R'lyeh Text* (Derleth's invention) or any text by a later Mythos author.

## 4. Two uses of text (decided)

| Kind | What it is | Attribution | Bound by the register? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temple text** | Most of what's on the walls: rites, prayers, warnings, puzzle clues. We write it for the game; in-world, the cult wrote it. | None. It's the cult's. | No. It's original to the game. |
| **Recognizable lines** | Real writings set into temple text so that players have phrases they know, or can search for, to anchor longer translations. They are there for meta reasons. | **None needed.** No in-world justification is needed either. They're easter eggs. | **No**, as long as they stay unattributed. |
| **Lore texts** | Texts presented *as sources*: an attributed inscription, a book or paper in the builder's collection, anything the professor cites | Required, and it must follow §2–3 | **Yes** |

*Eir's example:* a rite that ends with the last line of the Ring verse helps the player learn the script. It doesn't need to be attributed to Tolkien or to Sauron, and it doesn't need to be justified inside the temple.

**What follows from this** *(derived — confirm)*
- **Any attribution makes a line a lore text.** That includes a name, a speaker cue, or a framing like "as the old poet wrote". From then on the register applies, and it must never contradict another attribution.
- **Recognizable lines don't date the temple**, and they skip the channel check in §7.
- **The mythos rules still apply to recognizable lines.** Mythos names appear only in Lovecraft's own words and never in a taxonomy (§5). They are allowed in inscriptions anyway.
- **Rights still apply.** An easter egg is still a quotation, so the notes in Found Writings' "Rights and cautions" carry over. Tolkien and King are in copyright, and the Tolkien Estate does enforce. One short line in a rite is a small use, but check it before shipping.

## 5. The mythos in practice

**What is allowed**
- The names Lovecraft uses **in his own sentences**: Cthulhu, R'lyeh, Yog-Sothoth, Shub-Niggurath, Azathoth, Nyarlathotep, Tsathoggua, the Old Ones, Kadath, Hastur, the Lake of Hali, the Yellow Sign, the serpent-men of Valusia, the Shining Trapezohedron. Names Lovecraft borrowed from friends are fine **inside his sentences** and nowhere else.
- Quoting Lovecraft's in-world texts with their in-world attributions (Found Writings, "Lovecraft's invented texts").

**What is never allowed**
- Any taxonomy beyond Lovecraft's own words: no Elder Gods against Great Old Ones, no elemental affinities, no family trees, no good and evil sides. That rules out Derleth, the role-playing-game encyclopedias, and every later author.
- **Placing Rhys among them.** No authoritative voice ever says whether Rhys is one of the Old Ones, related to Yig or the serpent-men, older or younger than Cthulhu, or none of these. A character may *wonder*, uncertainly and in their own voice, as Lovecraft's narrators do. The game never answers.
- Explaining an entity. Mentions stay in passing.

**Where the mythos may appear (Eir)**

| Channel | Mythos references |
|---|---|
| Inscriptions in the script; in-game documents | Allowed |
| Professor dialogue over the **radio** | **Never**: no gods, places, books, Alhazred, nothing Lovecraft-related |
| Professor dialogue over the **telepathic link** | Allowed |
| Item names, menus, the notebook | **Never**, at any point in the game |

**The professor's gate costs nothing in play (Eir).** In the radio era he is mostly helping a lost student get out of the temple. Then the batteries die, and he is silent unless the player opens the telepathic link with the Knowledge Fang's active (§7.3 and §9.1 of the design). The gate falls where rule 2 needs it: scanning only opens with telepathy, and a player who has found a hidden Fang active is almost certainly reading the script and searching the lore already.

**The professor is a sceptic (Eir).** He distrusts plainly mythological accounts, so even after the gate he names people before gods. "Inspector Legrasse recorded a chant in 1907" comes easily to him; "Cthulhu" comes reluctantly, as the cultists' word and not his.

> **Proposal: the professor's university is not Miskatonic.** Naming it would break the gate in the first radio call. After telepathy he can mention Miskatonic's library as somewhere a scholar would go, but not as his own university.

## 6. Rhys and the cycle

Collected from `design_v2.md` §3 and §4 and from the ground rules.

- **Rhys'sthylceras** is an ancient snake deity with four fangs set in the roof of its mouth. It is searching throughout eternity for **a worthy vessel to summon it into the mortal realm and bring darkness to the world** (Eir).
- The four Fangs (Knowledge, Power, Life, Wealth) make their bearer a long-lived, super-strong, all-knowing monarch with limitless wealth. Every bearer goes mad, becomes obsessed with the earlier cults (rule 6), founds a cult, builds the next temple, and entombs themselves in it as sacrifice and guardian. Everyone who has held the Fangs has disappeared.
- **Only one temple of Rhys is active at a time.** Once its bearer is entombed, it stays hidden for a long time before revealing itself. The temple in this game is the previous bearer's tomb, and that bearer is the final guardian.
- Cults have appeared thousands of miles and hundreds of years apart. They share symbols and practices, and every temple carries the same script.
- **Rhys is not the only evil (Eir, rule 8).** Lovecraft's entities, Dracula, and whatever else the witnesses met are part of the same dark world, and none of them is explained as Rhys.
- **The Fangs have no hard history (Eir, rule 10).** No text, inscription or line from the professor ever puts the Fangs, a bearer or a temple of Rhys in a specific place or with a specific person, and above all not in the last few hundred years. The professor speaks of the cults in general terms (design §3.3).
- **At least one ancient Egyptian pyramid was a temple of Rhys (Eir).** This is the one fixed point in the cults' past. It is ancient, so it sits within rule 10.
  - *(derived)* If a past temple could still be standing thousands of years later, then a dead temple need not collapse or vanish. "One active temple at a time" therefore allows ruins of earlier ones. Design §3.2 should say so.
  - *(derived)* It gives the Egyptian sources a real anchor: Herodotus's and Pliny's Labyrinth, with its royal tombs that no one was allowed to see; Nitocris's underground chamber; "Ozymandias"; and Houdini's account of 1910, which is unreliable but possibly true (Eir, §3.2). The professor can bring them up after telepathy. He should still hedge about any single source, because the anchor is the pyramid, not the texts.

> **Proposal: leave the pyramid unnamed.** "At least one" invites the player to wonder which. Never naming it keeps rule 10's ambiguity and avoids pinning a known pharaoh (whose mummy may be in a museum) as a bearer. The professor can say something like "one of the pyramids — we don't know which — carried this script inside". He shouldn't name Giza, Saqqara or a particular king.

- **The Fangs moved from Egypt to the British Isles in the far distant past (Eir).** This is a behind-the-scenes truth, and it is one source of the ancient Arthurian legend: an ordinary man finds a magical object that declares him king. **It is never stated, and no single legend is "true".** Excalibur is not the Fangs. The link exists only as an inference that a very enterprising scholar who already knows about the Fangs could draw: the British Isles are full of stories of someone suddenly becoming a divine king. Arthurian legend has many layers, and each can be traced back through older stories, so the trail never resolves into one claim.

> **Proposal: the trail a scholar could follow.** These are all real legends, so they can be quoted as Legend sources (§3.4). None of them is claimed to be about the Fangs.
> - **The king-making object:** the sword in the stone. In Malory, "Whoso pulleth out this sword of this stone and anvil, is rightwise king born of all England". The Lia Fáil at Tara, the stone that cries out under the rightful king.
> - **From Egypt:** the medieval Irish and Scottish origin legends of **Scota**, a Pharaoh's daughter who came from Egypt to Ireland and Scotland. In the Scottish chronicles, the Stone of Destiny, the coronation stone, came with her. A real medieval legend already links Egypt to a king-making object in the British Isles, so the scholar's guess has something to build on.
> - **The king who doesn't die:** Arthur is carried to Avalon, the "once and future king" who sleeps under the hill. A bearer who vanishes into a tomb (§6) looks like this from outside.
>
>
> *Tone guardrail (§8):* keep to the kingship motif (sword, stone, Avalon). Leave the Grail alone, because it's a Christian sacred relic.

**Both the temple and the professor hint at the move (Eir).** The temple's collection places British material beside the Egyptian. The professor, after telepathy, notices what doesn't add up. The model case is ***The Faerie Queene*: Spenser's house of Mammon is unexpectedly accurate**, even though he wrote it in 1590, far later than anything it could have been drawn from. Spenser stays a real author and Guyon stays fictional. The accuracy is a true detail carried by fiction (§2.6), and nobody in-game ever says where Spenser got it.

> **Proposal: Prince Arthur is already in the poem.** Arthur rides through *The Faerie Queene*. In Book II, canto viii, it is Prince Arthur who arrives to defend Guyon when he faints after leaving Mammon's cave. The poem itself puts the Arthurian king beside the underground god of wealth, and a player who reads on finds the link without the game making it.

> **Proposal: how the professor voices it.** As a sceptic thinking aloud: "Spenser was writing allegory. He had no business getting the gold right… or the lamp." He never concludes anything.

**Open:**
- Is the pyramid ever named? Does the player learn of it from the professor, or from inscriptions? (See the proposal above.)
- **Vessel or vassal?** The ground rules say *vessel*, someone who summons Rhys into the world. `design_v2.md` §3.4 says *vassal* (in a Proposal block), and the True Bad ending makes the player Rhys's vassal, "the final Emperor of Humanity". These can be one idea, where the vessel summons and then serves, but the word matters for every text that describes the search.
- `design_v2.md` §3.4 still marks the worthy-candidate reading as a Proposal, while the ground rules treat it as given. If it's accepted, design_v2 should drop the Proposal marker.
- **How specific can the professor be about ancient cults?** Rule 10 is strictest for recent centuries. Egypt is now settled. Elsewhere, the *(derived)* default is that he can pass on ancient accounts that *might* concern a cult, but always hedged and never confirmed.

## 7. Time and channels

### 7.1 The temple's age (decided)
- **The temple is deliberately unplaceable.** It should feel thousands of years old, and no in-game source ever gives a construction date. Clues are allowed to disagree. The fact that it can't be placed is the point.
- **For us, the truth is that it couldn't have been built much before 1900.** That date is never stated in-game. It only limits what lore texts the temple can hold.
- **Old texts are fine whoever recorded them later.** The temple can quote Alhazred (c. 730) or the Pnakotic Manuscripts. It can carry any text the builder could have known, even if, in our world, we only know it through a later witness. When a lore text on a wall is credited, it is credited the way the builder would have done it ("a rite of the swamp cults", not "Legrasse, 1907").

### 7.2 Two channels (decided)
| Channel | What it carries | Date limit | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The temple** | Temple text, recognizable lines, and lore texts: attributed inscriptions, the builder's books and papers | Lore texts must predate the builder's entombment. Recognizable lines have no limit (§4). | In the script, or as physical objects |
| **The professor** | Anything scholars can get hold of: records, witness accounts, later scholarship, "an account from another explorer" | Up to the game's present | Plain text over the radio or the link. **Not** in the script. |

The professor knowing more than the builder did is intended. The later texts make the game feel grounded in history (Eir).

### 7.3 What may be inscribed (decided in part)
**Everything in Found Writings up to and including *The Faerie Queene* may be written in the script in the temple.** **Where the line falls after that is still open**, for the Arabic poets and the short lines from beyond the mythos, and will be tested in play.

*(derived)* That line matters only for **lore texts**. A passage inscribed as an attributed source must be one the builder could have had. The same words used as an unattributed recognizable line need no justification (§4).

**Open:**
- **When the builder was entombed.** Only lore texts depend on it, and it needs deciding only if an attributed 20th-century text goes on a wall. Blake's Trapezohedron passage (1935) is the likeliest case.
- Where the line falls after *The Faerie Queene*.

## 8. How the sources are used

**Decided (Eir, rules 8–9):** the witnesses really lived and their journeys really happened. Whether any of it touched Rhys is never specified, and the ambiguity helps. Two voices use these sources, each for their own reason:

- **The builder's collection** is obsession, not evidence. The builder gathered any scrap that might bear on Rhys, however tenuous. A text being in the temple says only that a madman thought it mattered.
- **The professor** is a source of knowledge for someone trying to escape a horror. He reaches for whatever fits the moment and stays general.

*Eir's examples of the professor:*
- When the player witnesses something terrifying: "We have accounts of great evils in the world. Van Helsing wrote that…"
- When the player begins to suspect the bearer lives on as an immortal guardian: "There are accounts by a scholar named Horace Holly of an ancient power giving eternal life to a devout follower."

**Source notes**

| Source | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Harker papers (Van Helsing) | The professor, on otherworldly forces and unknowable evil | Dracula is just Dracula (rule 8). Either channel works, because the papers predate the temple. |
| Quatermain's memoir | Temple (cleared, §7.3) or the professor | The journey was real, and any link to Rhys is unspecified. *Tone caution:* Haggard's Africa is a colonial fantasy. Found Writings already avoids his racial language; keep the Kukuana people as witnesses, never as villains of the piece. |
| Holly's manuscript (*She*) | The professor, on eternal life and devotion | Eir's example above |
| *The Faerie Queene* | Temple (cleared) and the professor | Mammon's house is unexpectedly accurate, a hint toward the Fangs in Britain (Eir, §6). The builder copied it, and the professor notices. Why it is accurate is never said. |
| "Ozymandias" | Temple or the professor | Eir's rule 3 example: it *may* be about a Rhys cult. That stays a possibility, never a claim. |
| Hamlet's soliloquy | Recognizable line (already transcribed) | > **Proposal:** place it where the player first realizes there's no way back up ("the undiscovered country from whose bourn no traveller returns"). |
| "The Raven" | Recognizable line (stanza 1 already transcribed) | Poe's alone. The narrator and Lenore never existed. |

> **Proposal: Pym's closing note as a hint.** The note reads the chasms of Tsalal as written words, their shapes seen from above forming roots that mean "to be shady" and "to be white". That is a landscape that spells words when you map it, which is the name puzzle of design §12. Placed as a lore text, it hints that a floor plan can be read, without claiming Tsalal had anything to do with Rhys. Poe stays the author (§2.6).

> **Proposal: Stoker's other "witness" books, for the professor.** By §2.3, *The Lair of the White Worm* (1911), an ancient serpent-woman in a pit in the English Midlands, and *The Jewel of Seven Stars* (1903), Queen Tera's planned resurrection, are also true accounts. They give the professor more general evidence: snake-things in the earth, the dead who arranged to return. Whether either touched Rhys is unspecified.

> **Proposal (from Found Writings): the Arabic poets.** Present them as human poets facing the dark, not as Rhys's cultists. That shows the dread is worldwide without repeating Lovecraft's sinister-Arab-sage trope. Alhazred is the one exception, and he is Lovecraft's.

> **Proposal: tone guardrail.** Real religions' sacred figures (Solomon, prophets, saints) are never made into Rhys cultists, and real atrocities are never re-explained as Rhys's doing.

## 10. Checklist for any new text

1. **Is it temple text, a recognizable line, or a lore text?** (§4) Original rites and unattributed lines can stop here, after the mythos check (step 4) and the rights check.
2. **For a lore text, who wrote it, and are they on the §3.1 list?** If not, the author is real (§2.2).
3. **If the author is real,** quote under their name, and never quote one of their invented characters as a speaker. A real person inside the work is still real (§2.4). **If it's Witness material,** quote the character, never the absent author, and remember the witness can be wrong (§2.5).
4. **Does it touch the mythos?** Then use only Lovecraft's own words and no taxonomy. It can go in inscriptions, in documents, or in the professor's mouth after telepathy, but never in items, menus or the notebook (§5).
5. **Which channel?** Lore texts in the temple must predate the builder's entombment. The professor can cite anything up to the game's present, in plain text (§7).
6. **Does it say anything about the Fangs' past?** Then cut it back to something general. No specific place or person, and above all nothing recent (rule 10).
7. **Does it contradict anything already attributed?** Then one of them has to change (rule 9).
8. **For a lore text, add it to the register** (§3) with its Basis.
