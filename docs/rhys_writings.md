# Found Writings — Lovecraft & Lore for the Script of Rhys

Oct 15, 2026 · @Eir

## How to use this

Every text below is wording a player can paste into a search engine, so a found document doubles as a Rosetta stone for the Script of Rhys. Each entry gives the text, the attribution line to print in-game, and the real source.

- **Fictional** = an author Lovecraft invented, attributed in-world. **Real** = a real author, printed as Lovecraft or the source gave it. **Mixed** = a real name over words that aren't quite theirs, which was Lovecraft's favourite trick.
- **Spell by sound.** The script writes sounds (§11.1), so archaic spellings like *shewed* or *suppos'd* cost nothing. Names like *Cthulhu* or *Yog-Sothoth* have no agreed pronunciation, so a player can't search them from sound alone. Keep a plain-English line near them.
- **No punctuation yet** (§11.4). Short lines read cleanly; the long Lamb or Machen sentences will need line breaks to carry their pauses.
- **Best first long texts:** the couplet, *In his house at R'lyeh dead Cthulhu waits dreaming*, and the Ozymandias pedestal. They're the most-searched lines here.
- **Language swap** (§14): the famous texts have standard translations in most languages, but translations carry their own copyright. See Rights and cautions.

Text is checked word for word against the [H.P. Lovecraft Archive](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/) (S. T. Joshi's corrected texts) and first printings. Where the first magazine printing differs, the note says so.

## Lovecraft's invented texts

These are Lovecraft's own words, presented in his stories as passages from in-world books. Print the in-world attribution; the source line is for you. Spelling variants between printings don't matter in-game, because the script spells sounds.

### The unexplainable couplet

Fictional · short · ["The Nameless City"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/nc.aspx) (written 1921)

> That is not dead which can eternal lie,\
And with strange aeons even death may die.

**Attribute to:** Abdul Alhazred, *Al Azif* (the *Necronomicon*), c. 730 A.D.

In "The Nameless City" Alhazred dreams of that city the night before he sings it. ["The Call of Cthulhu"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/cc.aspx) places it in the *Necronomicon*. *Weird Tales* (1928) printed "eons".

### The Old Ones were

Fictional · long (about 330 words) · ["The Dunwich Horror"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/dh.aspx) (*Weird Tales*, April 1929)

> Nor is it to be thought that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters, or that the common bulk of life and substance walks alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but between them, They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in Yog-Sothoth. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again. He knows where They have trod earth's fields, and where They still tread them, and why no one can behold Them as They tread. By Their smell can men sometimes know Them near, but of Their semblance can no man know, saving only in the features of those They have begotten on mankind; and of those are there many sorts, differing in likeness from man's truest eidolon to that shape without sight or substance which is Them. They walk unseen and foul in lonely places where the Words have been spoken and the Rites howled through at their Seasons. The wind gibbers with Their voices, and the earth mutters with Their consciousness. They bend the forest and crush the city, yet may not forest or city behold the hand that smites. Kadath in the cold waste hath known Them, and what man knows Kadath? The ice desert of the South and the sunken isles of Ocean hold stones whereon Their seal is engraven, but who hath seen the deep frozen city or the sealed tower long garlanded with seaweed and barnacles? Great Cthulhu is Their cousin, yet can he spy Them only dimly. Iä! Shub-Niggurath! As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold. Yog-Sothoth is the key to the gate, whereby the spheres meet. Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for here shall They reign again.

**Attribute to:** Abdul Alhazred, *Necronomicon*, in the Latin of Olaus Wormius (the 17th-century Spanish printing kept at Miskatonic University).

The story's interjection ("ran the text as Armitage mentally translated it") and Lovecraft's italics are removed. Three cuts stand on their own: *The Old Ones were … all are one in Yog-Sothoth* (the gate), *The ice desert of the South … barnacles* (engraved stones), and *Man rules now … reign again*, which mirrors the game's cycle.

### The nethermost caverns

Fictional · medium (about 140 words) · ["The Festival"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/f.aspx) (*Weird Tales*, January 1925)

> The nethermost caverns are not for the fathoming of eyes that see; for their marvels are strange and terrific. Cursed the ground where dead thoughts live new and oddly bodied, and evil the mind that is held by no head. Wisely did Ibn Schacabac say, that happy is the tomb where no wizard hath lain, and happy the town at night whose wizards are all ashes. For it is of old rumour that the soul of the devil-bought hastes not from his charnel clay, but fats and instructs the very worm that gnaws; till out of corruption horrid life springs, and the dull scavengers of earth wax crafty to vex it and swell monstrous to plague it. Great holes secretly are digged where earth's pores ought to suffice, and things have learnt to walk that ought to crawl.

**Attribute to:** Abdul Alhazred, *Necronomicon*, Olaus Wormius' Latin, quoting the sage Ibn Schacabac.

The best fit here for a descent into a temple. The name is "Schacabac" in both the Archive and *Weird Tales*; later reprints misprint it "Schacabao". *Weird Tales* reads "whose wizards are all in ashes".

### In his house at R'lyeh

Fictional · short · ["The Call of Cthulhu"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/cc.aspx) (*Weird Tales*, February 1928)

> Ph'nglui mglw'nafh Cthulhu R'lyeh wgah'nagl fhtagn.
>
> In his house at R'lyeh dead Cthulhu waits dreaming.

**Attribute to:** the chant of the Cthulhu cult, sung alike by Greenland Esquimau wizards and Louisiana swamp-priests. Its meaning was given by cult prisoners to Inspector John Raymond Legrasse of New Orleans, 1907.

Write the English line in the script; the chant has no fixed sound, so it works better as decoration beside it. A dead god dreaming under its house sits close to Rhys.

### Ever Their praises

Fictional · medium · ["The Whisperer in Darkness"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/wid.aspx) (*Weird Tales*, August 1931)

> … so from the wells of night to the gulfs of space, and from the gulfs of space to the wells of night, ever the praises of Great Cthulhu, of Tsathoggua, and of Him Who is not to be Named. Ever Their praises, and abundance to the Black Goat of the Woods. Iä! Shub-Niggurath! The Goat with a Thousand Young!

**Attribute to:** a ritual recorded on a phonograph by Henry Wentworth Akeley, about 1 a.m. on 1 May 1915, near a sealed cave mouth on Dark Mountain, Vermont.

Tsathoggua is Clark Ashton Smith's god, borrowed from his friend's then-unpublished story; the words are Lovecraft's. The full transcript breaks off mid-word, which suits a damaged document.

### What the Pnakotic Manuscripts tell

Fictional · short · ["The Other Gods"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/og.aspx) (written 1921, published 1933)

> Now it is told in the mouldy Pnakotic Manuscripts that Sansu found naught but wordless ice and rock when he climbed Hatheg-Kla in the youth of the world.

**Attribute to:** the Pnakotic Manuscripts of Lomar (cited by the chroniclers of Barzai the Wise).

Lovecraft only ever paraphrases the Manuscripts; this is the closest thing to a quotation.

### The brick cylinders of Kadatheron

Fictional · medium · ["The Doom That Came to Sarnath"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/ds.aspx) (*The Scot*, June 1920)

> It is written on the brick cylinders of Kadatheron that the beings of Ib were in hue as green as the lake and the mists that rise above it; that they had bulging eyes, pouting, flabby lips, and curious ears, and were without voice. It is also written that they descended one night from the moon in a mist; they and the vast still lake and grey stone city Ib.

**Attribute to:** the brick cylinders of Kadatheron.

The same story has a one-word inscription: Taran-Ish, high-priest of Sarnath, "had scrawled upon the altar of chrysolite with coarse shaky strokes the sign of DOOM." It suits an early, single-word reading.

### The Shining Trapezohedron

Fictional · short · ["The Haunter of the Dark"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/hd.aspx) (*Weird Tales*, December 1936)

> It was treasured and placed in its curious box by the crinoid things of Antarctica, salvaged from their ruins by the serpent-men of Valusia, and peered at aeons later in Lemuria by the first human beings.

**Attribute to:** no in-world author. In the story it's the narrator's account, so the attribution is yours to choose.

A relic passed from age to age, with serpent-men in its chain of keepers. The serpent-men of Valusia are Robert E. Howard's, named here by Lovecraft.

### The Dragon's Head and the Dragon's Tail

Fictional · short (incantation) · *The Case of Charles Dexter Ward* (written 1927, *Weird Tales* 1941)

> Y'AI 'NG'NGAH, YOG-SOTHOTH H'EE-L'GEB F'AI THRODOG UAAAH
>
> OGTHROD AI'F GEB'L-EE'H YOG-SOTHOTH 'NGAH'NG AI'Y ZHRO

**Attribute to:** the paired formulae of the wizard Joseph Curwen. The first (☊, the Dragon's Head) calls up; the second (☋, the Dragon's Tail) is the one Dr. Willett speaks to undo it.

The one text here that is already written as sound, so the script carries it exactly. A summoning and its reversal sit well beside a god you summon by name. Text as in the 1943 Arkham House edition; *Weird Tales* set each formula as a column ("Y'ai 'Ng'ngah / Yog-Sothoth / …"). Not yet checked against the Archive.

## Scholars and books to cite

Lovecraft's in-world history mixes invented scholars with real ones, often a real name given an invented deed. Dates below are from his essay ["History of the Necronomicon"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/hn.aspx) (written 1927) unless noted.

&#91;embedded content: The Necronomicon's in-world history, 730–1926\]

Wormius's Latin of 1228, in its 17th-century Spanish printing, is the text quoted in "The Dunwich Horror" and "The Festival". The essay never dates Dee's translation, so its bar spans his life.

## Real writings Lovecraft quoted

These are the real authors behind Lovecraft's epigraphs and quotations, so a player who finds one lands in the same library Lovecraft read. Text is as Lovecraft printed it unless noted.

### A reservoir of darkness

Real · medium · quoted in ["The Nameless City"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/nc.aspx), during the descent

> A reservoir of darkness, black\
As witches' cauldrons are, when fill'd\
With moon-drugs in th' eclipse distill'd.\
Leaning to look if foot might pass\
Down thro' that chasm, I saw, beneath,\
As far as vision could explore,\
The jetty sides as smooth as glass,\
Looking as if just varnish'd o'er\
With that dark pitch the Sea of Death\
Throws out upon its slimy shore.

**Attribute to:** Thomas Moore, *Alciphron* (1839), Letter IV.

The closest tie to your inspiration: the narrator of "The Nameless City" recites it going down the stairs. Moore's own printing differs by a few spellings ("caldrons", "filled", "through", "all smooth").

### The unreverberate blackness

Real · very short · chanted in ["The Nameless City"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/nc.aspx), on the same stairs

> the unreverberate blackness of the abyss

**Attribute to:** Lord Dunsany, "The Probable Adventure of the Three Literary Men" (*The Book of Wonder*, 1912).

Lovecraft's narrator chants it "over and over again" just before he recites Moore. Dunsany's sentence ends "…leaped over the edge of the World and is falling from us still through the unreverberate blackness of the abyss."

### The archetypes are in us

Real · long (about 150 words) · epigraph of ["The Dunwich Horror"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/dh.aspx)

> Gorgons, and Hydras, and Chimaeras—dire stories of Celaeno and the Harpies—may reproduce themselves in the brain of superstition—but they were there before. They are transcripts, types—the archetypes are in us, and eternal. How else should the recital of that which we know in a waking sense to be false come to affect us at all? Is it that we naturally conceive terror from such objects, considered in their capacity of being able to inflict upon us bodily injury? O, least of all! These terrors are of older standing. They date beyond body—or without the body, they would have been the same. . . . That the kind of fear here treated is purely spiritual—that it is strong in proportion as it is objectless on earth, that it predominates in the period of our sinless infancy—are difficulties the solution of which might afford some probable insight into our ante-mundane condition, and a peep at least into the shadowland of pre-existence.

**Attribute to:** Charles Lamb, "Witches and Other Night-Fears" (*The London Magazine*, 1821; collected in *Elia*, 1823).

Lovecraft trimmed and lightly reworded Lamb. The first two sentences stand alone well.

### Of such great powers

Real · medium · epigraph of ["The Call of Cthulhu"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/cc.aspx)

> Of such great powers or beings there may be conceivably a survival . . . a survival of a hugely remote period when . . . consciousness was manifested, perhaps, in shapes and forms long since withdrawn before the tide of advancing humanity . . . forms of which poetry and legend alone have caught a flying memory and called them gods, monsters, mythical beings of all sorts and kinds. . . .

**Attribute to:** Algernon Blackwood, *The Centaur* (1911), ch. X.

In Blackwood the "consciousness" is the Earth's own; Lovecraft cut "her" to make it his elder gods.

### Sacraments of evil

Real · medium · epigraph of ["The Horror at Red Hook"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/hrh.aspx)

> There are sacraments of evil as well as of good about us, and we live and move to my belief in an unknown world, a place where there are caves and shadows and dwellers in twilight. It is possible that man may sometimes return on the track of evolution, and it is my belief that an awful lore is not yet dead.

**Attribute to:** Arthur Machen, "The Red Hand" (1895).

### The charm to Hecate

Real · medium · the cult's rite in ["The Horror at Red Hook"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/hrh.aspx)

> O friend and companion of night, thou who rejoicest in the baying of dogs and spilt blood, who wanderest in the midst of shades among the tombs, who longest for blood and bringest terror to mortals, Gorgo, Mormo, thousand-faced moon, look favourably on our sacrifices!

**Attribute to:** an invocation of Hecate preserved by Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* IV.35 (3rd century). This English is E. B. Tylor's, in the article "Magic", *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 9th edition (1883).

Lovecraft copied it word for word from the *Britannica* and passed it off as his cult's rite: a real ancient prayer in a fictional mouth. Ready-made for a translated cult rite.

### Things which are not

Mixed · short · epigraph of ["The Festival"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/f.aspx)

> Efficiunt Daemones, ut quae non sunt, sic tamen quasi sint, conspicienda hominibus exhibeant.

**Attribute to:** Lactantius (as Lovecraft did). For an English document, use the real passage it paraphrases, *Divine Institutes* II.15, in William Fletcher's translation (1871):

> The art also and power of the Magi altogether consists in the influences of these; invoked by whom they deceive the sight of men with deceptive illusions, so that they do not see those things which exist, and think that they see those things which do not exist.

The Latin isn't Lactantius's own sentence. It's Nicolas Rémy's 1595 paraphrase, which Lovecraft found on the title page of Increase Mather's *Cases of Conscience* (1693).

### The essential Saltes

Mixed · medium · epigraph of *The Case of Charles Dexter Ward*

> The essential Saltes of Animals may be so prepared and preserved, that an ingenious Man may have the whole Ark of Noah in his own Studie, and raise the fine Shape of an Animal out of its Ashes at his Pleasure; and by the lyke Method from the essential Saltes of humane Dust, a Philosopher may, without any criminal Necromancy, call up the Shape of any dead Ancestour from the Dust whereinto his Bodie has been incinerated.

**Attribute to:** Borellus (as Lovecraft did).

The words are Cotton Mather's, paraphrasing Borel in *Pietas in Patriam* (1697), reprinted in *Magnalia Christi Americana* (1702). The archaic spellings are Lovecraft's own.

### That sinister adventure

Real · short · epigraph of ["Hypnos"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/hy.aspx)

> Apropos of sleep, that sinister adventure of all our nights, we may say that men go to bed daily with an audacity that would be incomprehensible if we did not know that it is the result of ignorance of the danger.

**Attribute to:** Charles Baudelaire, *Fusées* (in *Journaux intimes*, published 1887).

### Long be-nightmar'd

Real · short (verse) · epigraph of ["The Outsider"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/o.aspx)

> That night the Baron dreamt of many a woe,\
And all his warrior-guests, with shade and form\
Of witch, and demon, and large coffin-worm,\
Were long be-nightmar'd.

**Attribute to:** John Keats, "The Eve of St. Agnes" (1820), stanza XLII. Keats's 1820 text; Lovecraft has "woe;" and "be-nightmared".

### The Fates will find their way

Real · very short · epigraphs of "The Tree" and "The Tomb"

> Fata viam invenient.
>
> The Fates will find their way.

**Attribute to:** Virgil, *Aeneid* X.113 (also III.395); English by John Dryden (1697).

"The Tomb" opens with *Aeneid* VI.371, *Sedibus ut saltem placidis in morte quiescam*: "that at least in death I may rest in a peaceful abode", the drowned Palinurus begging for burial.

### Graven within the hills

Real · very short · Poe, whose *Pym* Lovecraft continued in *At the Mountains of Madness*

> I have graven it within the hills, and my vengeance upon the dust within the rock.

**Attribute to:** Edgar Allan Poe, *The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym* (1838), the closing note.

Made for a temple wall. The same book is the source of the cry *Tekeli-li!*, which Lovecraft's Antarctic horrors repeat.

### Out of space, out of time

Real · short (verse) · quoted in Lovecraft's essay *Supernatural Horror in Literature*

> By a route obscure and lonely,\
Haunted by ill angels only,\
Where an Eidolon, named NIGHT,\
On a black throne reigns upright,\
I have reached these lands but newly\
From an ultimate dim Thule —\
From a wild weird clime, that lieth, sublime,\
Out of SPACE — out of TIME.

**Attribute to:** Edgar Allan Poe, "Dream-Land" (*Graham's Magazine*, June 1844).

### The Book of Dzyan

Real · short · named in ["The Haunter of the Dark"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/hd.aspx)

> The eternal parent wrapped in her ever invisible robes had slumbered once again for seven eternities.
>
> Time was not, for it lay asleep in the infinite bosom of duration.

**Attribute to:** the Stanzas of Dzyan, Stanza I.1–2, from Helena Blavatsky, *The Secret Doctrine* (1888).

A real book that itself claimed to translate a lost ancient text, so it's "mixed" all the way down. Lovecraft shelved it beside the Pnakotic Manuscripts.

## Ancient and classic texts on lost temples

These are the most searchable texts here: famous, short, and about exactly what the player is crawling through. Each one has a thread back to Lovecraft, so they sit in the same world as the *Necronomicon* passages.

### The Labyrinth of Egypt

Real · medium (about 120 words)

> It has twelve courts covered in, with gates facing one another, six upon the North side and six upon the South, joining on one to another, and the same wall surrounds them all outside; and there are in it two kinds of chambers, the one kind below the ground and the other above upon these, three thousand in number, of each kind fifteen hundred. The upper set of chambers we ourselves saw, going through them, and we tell of them having looked upon them with our own eyes; but the chambers under ground we heard about only; for the Egyptians who had charge of them were not willing on any account to show them, saying that here were the sepulchres of the kings who had first built this labyrinth and of the sacred crocodiles.

**Attribute to:** Herodotus, *Histories* II.148, translated by G. C. Macaulay (1890). [Text](https://lexundria.com/hdt/2.148/mcly)

The section opens "This I saw myself, and I found it greater than words can say", if you want a longer version. The chambers nobody was allowed to see are the hook.

### Queen Nitocris's chamber

Real · medium (about 140 words)

> Of her they said that desiring to take vengeance for her brother, whom the Egyptians had slain when he was their king and then, after having slain him, had given his kingdom to her,—desiring, I say, to take vengeance for him, she destroyed by craft many of the Egyptians. For she caused to be constructed a very large chamber under ground, and making as though she would handsel it but in her mind devising other things, she invited those of the Egyptians whom she knew to have had most part in the murder, and gave a great banquet. Then while they were feasting, she let in the river upon them by a secret conduit of large size. Of her they told no more than this, except that, when this had been accomplished, she threw herself into a room full of embers, in order that she might escape vengeance.

**Attribute to:** Herodotus, *Histories* II.100, translated by G. C. Macaulay (1890). [Text](https://lexundria.com/hdt/2.100/mcly)

Lovecraft's Outsider dreams of "the unnamed feasts of Nitokris beneath the Great Pyramid", and ["Under the Pyramids"](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/up.aspx) retells this banquet. A flooded chamber also suits a water room.

### Atlantis sinks

Real · short (about 70 words)

> But afterwards there occurred violent earthquakes and floods; and in a single day and night of misfortune all your warlike men in a body sank into the earth, and the island of Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea. For which reason the sea in those parts is impassable and impenetrable, because there is a shoal of mud in the way; and this was caused by the subsidence of the island.

**Attribute to:** Plato, *Timaeus* 25c–d, translated by Benjamin Jowett. [Text](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-greece/plato/plato-timaeus.asp?pg=10)

The Egyptian priest is telling Solon of a lost elder age, much as Lovecraft's narrators learn of theirs.

### Ozymandias

Real · medium (14 lines)

> I met a traveller from an antique land,\
Who said: Two vast and trunkless legs of stone\
Stand in the desart. Near them, on the sand,\
Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown,\
And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,\
Tell that its sculptor well those passions read\
Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,\
The hand that mocked them and the heart that fed:\
And on the pedestal these words appear:\
"My name is Ozymandias, king of kings:\
Look on my works, ye Mighty, and despair!"\
Nothing beside remains. Round the decay\
Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare\
The lone and level sands stretch far away.

**Attribute to:** Percy Bysshe Shelley, "Ozymandias" (*The Examiner*, 1818; text of *Rosalind and Helen*, 1819).

The best first long text in the game: almost every English speaker knows the two pedestal lines. A god-king who wanted to be remembered also suits a Fang-bearer's tomb.

### The City in the Sea

Real · short (verse)

> Lo! Death has reared himself a throne\
In a strange city lying alone\
Far down within the dim West,\
Where the good and the bad and the worst and the best\
Have gone to their eternal rest.\
There shrines and palaces and towers\
(Time-eaten towers that tremble not!)\
Resemble nothing that is ours.\
Around, by lifting winds forgot,\
Resignedly beneath the sky\
The melancholy waters lie.

**Attribute to:** Edgar Allan Poe, "The City in the Sea" (*The Raven and Other Poems*, 1845), first stanza. [Text](https://eapoe.org/WORKS/poems/cityseae.htm)

Lovecraft's essay on Poe names its "shocking spires and domes under the sea".

### Many-Columned Iram

Real · medium (about 110 words)

> Now when I came to the castle, I found it had two vast gates (never in the world was seen their like for size height) inlaid with all manner of jewels and jacinths, white and red, yellow and green. Beholding this I marvelled with great marvel and thought the case mighty wondrous; then entering the citadel in a flutter of fear and dazed with surprise and affright, I found it long and wide, about equalling Al-Medinah in point of size; and therein were lofty palaces laid out in pavilions all built of gold and silver and inlaid with many-coloured jewels and jacinths and chrysolites and pearls.

**Attribute to:** *The Thousand Nights and a Night*, "The City of Many-Columned Iram and Abdullah Son of Abi Kilabah" (Night 276), translated by Richard F. Burton (1885).

"For size height", with no "and", is how Burton printed it. "The Nameless City" calls the man killed in its last mural "perhaps a pioneer of ancient Irem, the City of Pillars", and Lovecraft named Alhazred after reading the *Nights* as a boy.

### Thunder behind the doors

Real · short (one sentence)

> Some of the palaces are so peculiarly constructed, that the moment the doors are opened a dreadful sound like that of thunder reverberates within: the greater part, too, of these edifices have to be traversed in total darkness.

**Attribute to:** Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* XXXVI.19, translated by John Bostock and H. T. Riley (1855). [Text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=36:chapter=19)

The same Egyptian labyrinth Herodotus walked, described by a second ancient writer. Two sources that agree make a nice cross-check for the professor.

## King Solomon's Mines

H. Rider Haggard's 1885 novel is the lost-civilisation adventure this genre grew from, and it's public domain everywhere (Haggard died in 1925), so it needs no fair-use argument. It even opens with a found document: a dying explorer's map and letter, written in his own blood. Text is the [Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2166). Haggard's narrator uses the racial language of his time; these passages avoid it, and one note says where I cut.

### The dying explorer's letter

Fictional (Haggard's) · long (about 230 words) · ch. II

> I, Jose da Silvestra, who am now dying of hunger in the little cave where no snow is on the north side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I have named Sheba's Breasts, write this in the year 1590 with a cleft bone upon a remnant of my raiment, my blood being the ink. If my slave should find it when he comes, and should bring it to Delagoa, let my friend (name illegible) bring the matter to the knowledge of the king, that he may send an army which, if they live through the desert and the mountains, and can overcome the brave Kukuanes and their devilish arts, to which end many priests should be brought, will make him the richest king since Solomon. With my own eyes I have seen the countless diamonds stored in Solomon's treasure chamber behind the white Death; but through the treachery of Gagool the witch-finder I might bring nought away, scarcely my life. Let him who comes follow the map, and climb the snow of Sheba's left breast till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which is the great road Solomon made, from whence three days' journey to the King's Palace. Let him kill Gagool. Pray for my soul. Farewell.

**Attribute to:** José da Silvestra, Portuguese explorer, 1590.

The novel prints his Portuguese original in a footnote, so this is a ready-made two-language text. If the mountains' name isn't the tone you want, the cut from "With my own eyes" to "Farewell" stands on its own.

### Out of the dark we came

Fictional speaker · medium · ch. V

> Out of the dark we came, into the dark we go. Like a storm-driven bird at night we fly out of the Nowhere; for a moment our wings are seen in the light of the fire, and, lo! we are gone again into the Nowhere. Life is nothing. Life is all. It is the Hand with which we hold off Death. It is the glow-worm that shines in the night-time and is black in the morning; it is the white breath of the oxen in winter; it is the little shadow that runs across the grass and loses itself at sunset.

**Attribute to:** Umbopa, the hidden heir to the Kukuana throne. His speech opens "What is life?"

### I am old

Fictional speaker · medium · ch. IX, at the witch-hunt

> Blood! blood! blood! rivers of blood; blood everywhere. I see it, I smell it, I taste it—it is salt! it runs red upon the ground, it rains down from the skies.
>
> I am old! I am old! I have seen much blood; ha, ha! but I shall see more ere I die, and be merry. How old am I, think ye? Your fathers knew me, and their fathers knew me, and their fathers' fathers' fathers. I have seen the white man and know his desires. I am old, but the mountains are older than I. Who made the great road, tell me? Who wrote the pictures on the rocks, tell me? Who reared up the three Silent Ones yonder, that gaze across the pit, tell me?

**Attribute to:** Gagool, the ancient witch-finder of the Kukuanas.

Unknown builders and pictures on the rocks: close to your temple. I stopped where her answer turns to the period's myth that a vanished white race built Africa's ruins.

### The Silent Ones

Narration · medium · ch. XVI

> That to our left was serene in countenance, but the calm upon it seemed dreadful. It was the calm of that inhuman cruelty, Sir Henry remarked, which the ancients attributed to beings potent for good, who could yet watch the sufferings of humanity, if not without rejoicing, at least without sorrow. These three statues form a most awe-inspiring trinity, as they sit there in their solitude, and gaze out across the plain for ever.

**Attribute to:** Allan Quatermain, of the three colossi at the mouth of the mines.

Made for a statue base. Just before this, the right-hand figure "had the face of a devil".

### The White Death

Narration · medium · ch. XVI, "The Place of Death"

> There at the end of the long stone table, holding in his skeleton fingers a great white spear, sat Death himself, shaped in the form of a colossal human skeleton, fifteen feet or more in height. High above his head he held the spear, as though in the act to strike; one bony hand rested on the stone table before him, in the position a man assumes on rising from his seat, whilst his frame was bent forward so that the vertebrae of the neck and the grinning, gleaming skull projected towards us, and fixed its hollow eye-places upon us, the jaws a little open, as though it were about to speak.

**Attribute to:** Allan Quatermain.

Around the table sit twenty-seven dead kings, slowly turned to stalactite by dripping water, "iced over and preserved for ever". Dead kings kept for ever at Death's table are close kin to your Fang-bearers. The chapter ends "Such, at any rate, was the White Death and such were the White Dead!"

## Dracula

Its two most famous lines, the welcome and "the children of the night", are already in the short-lines table below; these are the monstrous ones. Text is the [Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/345) of the 1897 novel.

### What the vampire is

Real · long (about 150 words) · ch. XVIII, in Mina Harker's journal

> The nosferatu do not die like the bee when he sting once. He is only stronger; and being stronger, have yet more power to work evil. This vampire which is amongst us is of himself so strong in person as twenty men; he is of cunning more than mortal, for his cunning be the growth of ages; he have still the aids of necromancy, which is, as his etymology imply, the divination by the dead, and all the dead that he can come nigh to are for him at command; he is brute, and more than brute; he is devil in callous, and the heart of him is not; he can, within limitations, appear at will when, and where, and in any of the forms that are to him; he can, within his range, direct the elements; the storm, the fog, the thunder; he can command all the meaner things: the rat, and the owl, and the bat—the moth, and the fox, and the wolf; he can grow and become small; and he can at times vanish and come unknown.

**Attribute to:** Professor Abraham Van Helsing.

The broken English is Stoker's, and the script spells sound, so it reads naturally on a wall. "Cunning … the growth of ages" also fits a god who has outlasted every bearer.

### If we fail

Real · medium · ch. XVIII, the same speech

> For if we fail in this our fight he must surely win; and then where end we? Life is nothings; I heed him not. But to fail here, is not mere life or death. To us for ever are the gates of heaven shut; for who shall open them to us again? We go on for all time abhorred by all; a blot on the face of God's sunshine; an arrow in the side of Him who died for man.

**Attribute to:** Professor Abraham Van Helsing.

Your Bad ending in one paragraph: fail, and you become the next monster.

### Time is on my side

Real · short · ch. XXIII

> My revenge is just begun! I spread it over centuries, and time is on my side. Your girls that you all love are mine already; and through them you and others shall yet be mine—my creatures, to do my bidding and to be my jackals when I want to feed.

**Attribute to:** Count Dracula, to his hunters.

## The Faerie Queene

Spenser's epic (Books I–III 1590, I–VI 1596) is public domain everywhere, and its stanza is a decipherment gift. Every stanza has nine lines: eight of five beats, then a longer ninth, rhyming *ababbcbcc*. In a phonetic script, rhyming words end in the same glyphs, so a player can see the line-endings repeat in that fixed pattern and the last line run long. That is enough to say "this is verse", and an English student may even say "this is Spenser".

Text is the 1596 old spelling, where u and v swap (*siluer* is "silver"). The script spells sound, so on the wall it reads as plain English, and the modern spelling is easy to search.

### The first stanza

Real · one stanza · Book I, Canto I, stanza 1

> A Gentle Knight was pricking on the plaine,\
Y cladd in mightie armes and siluer shielde,\
Wherein old dints of deepe wounds did remaine,\
The cruell markes of many' a bloudy fielde;\
Yet armes till that time did he neuer wield:\
His angry steede did chide his foming bitt,\
As much disdayning to the curbe to yield:\
Full iolly knight he seemd, and faire did sitt,\
As one for knightly giusts and fierce encounters fitt.

**Attribute to:** Edmund Spenser, *The Faerie Queene* I.i.1.

The most-quoted stanza in the poem, and the easiest one to search.

### The house of gold

A cave of gold under dust, lit like a dying lamp. It could be the Wealth region's establishing text.

## Ancient Arabic poets

Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry is some of the oldest poetry still widely read: the *Mu'allaqat*, or "Suspended Odes", date from the 6th century and are said to have hung at the Kaaba. It's full of this game's material: ruined campsites read like old inscriptions, night as a crushing weight, and death striking blind. These are real poets in public-domain translations, mostly Reynold A. Nicholson's *A Literary History of the Arabs* (1907). One suggestion: present them as what they are, human poets facing the dark, rather than as Rhys's cultists. That shows the dread is worldwide without repeating Lovecraft's sinister-Arab-sage trope.

### Like weather-beaten script

Real · medium · the opening of Labid's *Mu'allaqa*

> Waste lies the land where once alighted and did wone\
The people of Miná: Rijám and Ghawl are lone.\
The camp in Rayyán's vale is marked by relics dim\
Like weather-beaten script engraved on ancient stone.
>
> I stopped and asked, but what avails it that we ask\
Dumb changeless things that speak a language all unknown?

**Attribute to:** Labid ibn Rabi'a (c. 560–c. 661), *Mu'allaqa*, translated by R. A. Nicholson (1907).

The best fit in this whole doc for a script-reading game: ruins compared to worn inscriptions, then stones "that speak a language all unknown". The full passage is 18 lines; between the two parts above, 12 lines of rain and grazing animals are left out.

### O hideous night

Real · medium · Imru' al-Qais's *Mu'allaqa*, the night passage

> Often has the night drawn her skirts around me, like the billows of the ocean, to make trial of my fortitude in a variety of cares;\
And I said to her, when she seemed to extend her sides, to drag on her unwieldy length, and to advance slowly with her breast:\
"Dispel thy gloom, O tedious night! that the morn may rise; although my sorrows are such, that the morning-light will not give more comfort than thy shades.\
"O hideous night!—a night in which the stars are prevented from rising, as if they were bound to a solid cliff with strong cables!"

**Attribute to:** Imru' al-Qais (d. c. 540), *Mu'allaqa*, translated by Sir William Jones (1782).

Imru' al-Qais is usually called the greatest of the pre-Islamic poets. Night as a beast pinning the stars to a cliff suits a region where the lantern dies.

### The ballad of vengeance

Real · short · the last lines of Ta'abbata Sharran's ballad

> O'er the fallen of Hudhayl stands screaming\
The hyena; see the wolf's teeth gleaming!\
Dawn will hear the flap of wings, will discover\
Vultures treading corpses, too gorged to hover.

**Attribute to:** Ta'abbata Sharran (6th century), translated by R. A. Nicholson (1907).

His name means "he put a mischief under his arm-pit"; one story says the mischief was a Ghoul he had killed and carried home. Some scholars think this ballad is a later forgery, which suits a doubtful in-world document.

### Bury me not

Real · short · al-Shanfara

> Bury me not! Me you are forbidden to bury,\
But thou, O hyena, soon wilt feast and make merry,\
When foes bear away mine head, wherein is the best of me,\
And leave on the battle-field for thee all the rest of me.

**Attribute to:** al-Shanfara (6th century), the outlaw poet, translated by R. A. Nicholson (1907).

### Shattered like glass

Real · short · Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri

> We laugh, but inept is our laughter;\
We should weep and weep sore,\
Who are shattered like glass, and thereafter\
Re-moulded no more!

**Attribute to:** Abu al-Ala al-Ma'arri (973–1057), translated by R. A. Nicholson (1907).

Al-Ma'arri was the great pessimist of Arabic poetry, a blind poet-philosopher. Ameen Rihani's freer 1918 version of his "tread lightly" theme is in the table below.

## Rights and cautions

Everything here should be safe to ship, with the softest spots being Lovecraft's 1931–1941 publications in the US and any translation you use for the language swap. I'm not a lawyer; this is the factual picture to check against.

- **Lovecraft died in 1937**, so his work is public domain wherever the term is life + 70 (since 2008). In the US, anything published in 1930 or earlier is public domain: "The Nameless City", "The Festival", "The Call of Cthulhu", "The Dunwich Horror", "The Doom That Came to Sarnath", the Red Hook, Outsider and Hypnos epigraphs.
- **US 1931–1941 publications** ("The Whisperer in Darkness", "The Other Gods", "The Haunter of the Dark", *Charles Dexter Ward*, "History of the Necronomicon") rest on the copyrights never having been renewed. They're widely treated as public domain, and Standard Ebooks publishes "The Other Gods", "The Haunter of the Dark" and Charles Dexter Ward as such, but lean on the pre-1931 texts for anything load-bearing. "Whisperer" (1931) enters the US public domain on 1 January 2027 regardless.
- **Which wording.** The Archive uses S. T. Joshi's corrected texts; the first printings are the most conservative choice, and every difference is noted under its entry. Because the script is phonetic, almost none of them change what's written on the wall.
- **Every other author** died more than 70 years ago, and every translation here is 19th-century or older (Macaulay, Jowett, Burton, Bostock and Riley, Fletcher, Dryden, Tylor).
- **Lord Dunsany.** "The Nameless City" chants his phrase "the unreverberate blackness of the abyss" (*The Book of Wonder*, 1912). It's public domain in the US. Dunsany died in 1957, so in the UK and EU his work is protected until 1 January 2028; until then a phrase rests on those countries' quotation exceptions, which are narrower than US fair use. Six words on a wall is about as small as a quotation gets.
- **Translations carry their own copyright.** For the language swap (§14), a modern French or German Lovecraft is the translator's work. Use an old public-domain translation or commission your own.
- **Left out: later Mythos writers.** Nothing by Derleth or anyone after Lovecraft. The *R'lyeh Text* is Derleth's invention and Lovecraft never quotes it. Tsathoggua and the serpent-men of Valusia appear only as names inside Lovecraft's own sentences.
- **Worth knowing, also left out:** Lovecraft ghost-wrote "The Curse of Yig" (1929), a story about a serpent god, "Father of Serpents". It was published under Zealia Bishop's name, so it doesn't fit a "Lovecraft quote" rule, but it's the closest thing in his work to Rhys.

**Not fully checked:** the Dragon's Head and Tail wording against the Archive, and which Jowett edition (1871 or 1892) the Timaeus text follows.
